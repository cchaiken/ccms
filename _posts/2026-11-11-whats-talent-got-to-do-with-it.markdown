I recently watched a video of a boy who was featured in a news segment on television. His claim to fame was producing his own recordings of cover songs, singing the vocals and playing every instrument heard on the original tracks. He started playing different instruments by age 5, and at the tender age of 9, he is receiving accolades from world-renowned music producers and artists for his multitrack productions.
The question is, how much of this is talent and how much is skill? And what is the difference? 

Simply put: Talent is innate, Skill is learned.

Very rarely do I meet someone who cannot learn the basics of singing: breath control and pitch recognition. With practice, you can hone your musical skills, gain range, sharpen your ear, build stamina, and learn to sing with ease and confidence. With consistent practice, pianists learn to coordinate both hands to work in tandem, move around the keys with ease, and advance to smooth (legato) movements and phrasing. When we speak, we naturally use our “singing techniques,” relying on breath control and the rhythm of speech, so chances are we will find that singing is more intuitive and easier to learn. Likewise, anybody who took a few required typing lessons in school learned to move freely around the “other keyboard,” using both hands at the same time and combining hand-eye coordination: the foundations of piano playing!

So what is the difference between a person with innate biological or cognitive traits and someone who has the discipline to learn both physical and mental techniques? Well, that’s the fun part for us to observe and experience.

So how much was talent, and how much was skill? Probably both, and they feed each other. Talent may be the spark, but practice is the fuel. That boy didn’t just wake up playing all those instruments. He found the time, trusted his ear, and learned through trial and error. And that’s the encouraging part: whatever your starting point, you can learn to sing and play, as long as you’re willing to practice.

Everyone is musical. Practice brings out the music.
